# AI Capital Spending — Reconciling the Model's Anchor with Headline Figures

**What this is.** ATLAS anchors its 2025 measurement of United States AI buildout
investment at $130–155 billion, with $140 billion as the working point. Public
discussion of AI capital spending routinely quotes figures three to five times
larger. Both are defensible numbers about different questions. This note bridges
them, line by line, so a reader comparing the model's anchor against a headline
figure can see exactly which differences in geography, timing, measurement basis,
and scope account for the gap. The model's forward spending path is generated by its
own financing and demand machinery; announced corporate spending plans never anchor
it — they belong to scenario analysis, and to this note as context.

## The model's anchor, as constructed

| Component | Value (2025) | Source and vintage |
|---|---|---|
| Data-center construction, private, value put in place | **$49.7B** | U.S. Census Bureau, Construction Spending (C30), data-center line, 2025 annual; fetched 2026-08-17 |
| AI-attributable computing-equipment investment | **$80–105B** | Bureau of Economic Analysis, National Income and Product Accounts Table 5.3.5 (computers & peripheral equipment: $270.7B actual 2025) minus a pre-boom trend counterfactual ($167–190B, from the 2019–23 growth path); the excess is the boom attribution |
| **Total** | **$130–155B** | floor-leaning by construction: AI software and R&D intangibles, power and communication structures, and AI servers recorded under other equipment lines are excluded because they cannot be cleanly separated at published line detail |

Scale: roughly 2.4–2.8% of 2025 U.S. private fixed investment ($5,443.5B), or
0.42–0.50% of GDP.

## The bridge to headline figures

| Difference | Headline basis | The model's basis | Effect on the gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Geography** | Global spending by U.S.-headquartered firms (overseas data centers, foreign chip fabrication) | United States domestic investment only (the national accounts' boundary) | Headline larger — a substantial share of hyperscaler capital spending lands abroad |
| **Year and basis** | Forward guidance and announcements (2026 commitments; multi-year programs quoted as single figures) | 2025 value actually put in place / equipment actually delivered | Headline larger — announcements lead spending, and some announced programs are never fully built |
| **Scope** | Total capital expenditure of AI-heavy firms (including non-AI cloud, networking, office, vehicles) or "AI-related" spending broadly defined | AI-attributable spending within two national-accounts categories | Headline larger — total capex is not AI capex |
| **Import content** | Purchases counted at full value regardless of origin | National accounts net out imported content from GDP via the trade balance; an accelerator fabricated in Taiwan adds to U.S. investment but is offset in net exports | Headline overstates the domestic production stimulus. **A known model limitation, documented:** the model currently applies no import offset to its AI equipment investment, so its domestic stimulus from the buildout is overstated by the imported-content share; the planned correction applies a cited import-content share from the BEA input-output import matrices so that domestic stimulus = construction + power + domestically produced equipment + distribution margins, with imported silicon neutral to GDP |

## How the model's own path compares (2026–27, generated, not tuned)

The model's financing machinery (retained profits of the AI-building firms plus
credit) and its capacity-gap demand generate: 2026 ≈ $123B (demand-limited; financing
capacity ≈ $176B was not exhausted) and 2027 ≈ $209B (financing-limited). Against the
observed-world 2026 class of roughly $170–220B (the Census construction line running
~$61B annualized in early 2026 plus equipment growth), the model's 2026 under-runs
modestly: its demand derives from closing a believed capability-capacity gap at
measured unit costs, while observed spending includes anticipatory overbuild financed
against expectations. The comparison is reported each run and never forced to match:
a persistent large divergence is a prompt to re-examine the financing machinery's
measured inputs, not to copy announcements.
